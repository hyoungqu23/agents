# English line-editing notes

Use these notes only for English passages. They supplement the core skill; they do not override a house style, quoted language, a character's speech, or the user's deliberate voice.

## Common English signals

Inspect, rather than automatically delete, phrases in these families:

- Announced openings: “here is the thing,” “let us explore,” “in today's world,” “it is worth noting.”
- Manufactured revelation: “the truth is,” “what everyone misses,” “the real question,” “plot twist.”
- Inflated consequence: “a pivotal moment,” “a testament to,” “changes everything,” “sets a new standard.”
- Corporate fog: “leverage,” “facilitate,” “robust,” “seamless,” “transformative,” “holistic,” when a simpler word preserves the meaning.
- Empty transitions: “additionally,” “moreover,” “with that in mind,” when the relation is already clear or the phrase supplies no relation.
- Vague attribution: “experts agree,” “research suggests,” “many believe,” without a named source.
- Weak predicate substitutes: “serves as,” “stands as,” “has the ability to,” when “is,” “has,” or a concrete action is clearer.
- Tacked-on interpretation: participial endings that label what a fact supposedly demonstrates instead of explaining a cause or result.
- Generic finishers: broad forecasts, motivational summaries, and polished mic-drop lines unsupported by the body.

Words are not guilty on sight. Keep one when it is technically exact, appears in a quotation, matches the writer's established diction, or performs a necessary logical job.

## Clarity checks

- Make the subject, verb, and object easy to locate. Move long interruptions when the reader must hold too much before reaching the verb.
- Put modifiers near what they modify. Repair dangling introductory phrases and ambiguous pronouns.
- Fix comma splices and accidental fragments, but preserve intentional fragments that are clear in context.
- Prefer active voice when it identifies responsibility or sharpens action. Passive voice is often right for methods, unknown actors, and sentences whose topic is the receiver.
- Use positive form when it states the claim more directly. Keep negative wording for real denial, prohibition, exception, or contrast.
- Keep one main purpose per paragraph. Expository paragraphs often benefit from early orientation; narrative and persuasive prose may delay the point deliberately.
- Place new or emphatic information where the sentence gives it appropriate weight, often near the end. Do not append a fake-profound kicker merely to occupy that position.

## Rhythm and punctuation

- Repeated short declarations can sound staged. Repeated long compound sentences can sound mechanical. Change the underlying structure, not just the word count.
- An em dash is not evidence of AI authorship. Keep it when it marks a useful interruption or turn better than nearby alternatives; remove clusters and decorative use.
- Colons work for lists, definitions, explanations, and quotations. Recast a colon used only to manufacture a reveal.
- Parentheses can hold genuinely secondary information. Delete them when they become an escape hatch for thoughts that deserve integration or removal.
- Use sentence case or title case according to the publication's style. Do not infer authorship from capitalization alone.
- Use straight or curly quotation marks according to the target system and house style.

## Voice preservation

Before editing, notice contractions, sentence length, regional usage, humor, directness, profanity, and tolerance for fragments. Do not replace a distinctive ordinary word with a polished synonym. Repeat the same noun when it refers to the same thing.

If no English house style is supplied, prioritize current, idiomatic usage over prescriptions that are dated, disputed, or specific to early twentieth-century formal prose.
