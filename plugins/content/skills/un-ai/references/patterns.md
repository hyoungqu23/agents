# Pattern guide

Use this guide only when `SKILL.md` routes here for a broad language-neutral audit or an unresolved cross-language pattern. It is not a default companion to routine English or Korean editing. A pattern is evidence for inspection, not an automatic deletion rule. Diagnose what the construction is doing in context.

## The decision test

Before changing a suspected pattern, ask:

1. Does it carry a fact, logical relation, honest qualification, genre convention, or recognizable voice?
2. Would removing it alter the claim or cadence in a useful way?
3. Is the problem the construction itself, or the absence of evidence behind it?
4. Can the revision use only information the user supplied?

Keep the construction if it earns its place. If the source lacks the fact needed for a real fix, flag the gap instead of writing a more polished empty sentence.

Do not confuse **present in the draft** with **supported by evidence**. Preserve core meaning and material commitments, but a redundant evaluation, slogan, or ceremonial promise remains editable even when the writer supplied it. Cutting empty rhetoric is different from inventing or reversing a claim.

## Substance patterns

### Portable claims

**Signal:** A sentence could describe almost any team, product, policy, or person.

**Typical effect:** It occupies space without helping this reader understand this subject.

**Fix:** Replace the category with a supplied instance, mechanism, comparison, number, name, decision, or consequence. Otherwise cut it or mark the missing detail.

### Importance inflation

**Signal:** The prose labels an ordinary event pivotal, transformative, essential, unprecedented, or profound without showing why.

**Fix:** State what happened and what changed. Let the consequence establish importance.

**Keep when:** The term has a defined threshold in the domain and the evidence meets it.

### Unsupported attribution

**Signal:** Unnamed experts, studies, reports, critics, users, or observers support a claim.

**Fix:** Name and cite the source, qualify the claim accurately, or remove it. Ask for a source when the claim matters.

### Superficial explanation

**Signal:** A trailing clause labels significance instead of supplying a cause or result, often with words equivalent to “showing,” “highlighting,” or “reflecting.”

**Fix:** Give the actual mechanism or consequence. Delete the clause if the sentence already stands.

### Fake precision

**Signal:** Numbers, anecdotes, quotations, or named examples appear plausible but are not in the source material.

**Fix:** Remove them and expose the factual gap. Specificity must be true.

### Confidence mismatch

**Signal:** The prose either buries a supported conclusion under stacked hedges or upgrades uncertainty into certainty.

**Fix:** Match confidence to evidence. Keep meaningful terms such as “may,” “likely,” or “I think” when they encode real uncertainty or stance.

### Evidence-relation drift

**Signal:** The revision turns sequence, correlation, association, attribution, or a single example into causation or a general rule.

**Fix:** Preserve the relationship the source actually supports. Use causal language only when the evidence or source states a causal basis. Keep denominators, comparison groups, time windows, and scope when they affect interpretation.

## Rhetorical patterns

### Throat-clearing and self-announcement

**Signal:** The text announces that it will explain, explore, clarify, or reveal before making the point.

**Fix:** Start with the point unless the setup creates necessary context, tension, or character.

### Hollow rejected-frame contrast

**Signal:** The writer rejects X to make Y sound revelatory: “not X but Y,” “less X, more Y,” or a question immediately answered with a reversal.

**Fix:** State Y directly.

**Keep only when all apply:**

- The reader is likely to hold X or X is necessary context.
- Y is concrete enough to test, picture, or act on.
- The body supports Y rather than merely repeating it.
- Contrast is the clearest relation, not a drama device.

### Faux-insider setup

**Signal:** The writer claims everyone misses, ignores, or misunderstands the next point.

**Fix:** Remove the status claim and test whether the point survives. Supply evidence if prevalence matters.

### Rhetorical question-answer pair

**Signal:** A question exists only to cue the writer's prepared answer.

**Fix:** State the answer. Keep a real question when it frames an unresolved issue, involves the reader, or organizes an interview or FAQ.

### Dramatic reveal punctuation

**Signal:** A colon, dash, or line break delays a thin reveal.

**Fix:** Write a normal sentence or combine the clauses. Keep the punctuation for lists, definitions, quotations, genuine explanations, or a deliberate voice beat.

### Negative runway

**Signal:** Several fragments list what a thing is not before naming it.

**Fix:** Lead with what it is. Keep exclusions when they prevent a real misunderstanding or define scope.

### Forced symmetry

**Signal:** A list has an artificial count, several clauses share a slogan rhythm, or paragraphs repeat an identical template.

**Fix:** Use the number and shape demanded by the content. Parallel grammar is useful when the ideas truly have equal status; empty symmetry is not.

### False range

**Signal:** “From X to Y” joins endpoints that do not form a meaningful scale or omits the middle entirely.

**Fix:** Name the actual scope or list the categories directly.

### Repeated conclusion

**Signal:** The ending summarizes what the reader just read or adds a generic prediction, uplift, warning, or aphorism.

**Fix:** End on the last concrete consequence, decision, image, or next action. Keep a recap when the format requires one or the piece is long enough that retrieval matters.

If the draft ends with a broad pledge, distinguish a real commitment from ceremony. Preserve a commitment that creates an obligation or changes what the reader should expect. Cut a generic aspiration that could close any post. Do not turn it into a concrete plan without authorization.

## Sentence and word patterns

### Inflated diction

**Signal:** A prestigious or fashionable word makes the subject sound impressive without adding a distinction.

**Fix:** Use the ordinary precise word. Do not replace a correct technical term merely because it appears on a blocklist.

### Weak verb phrase or nominalization

**Signal:** The sentence hides the action in a noun or uses a long phrase where a direct verb would be clearer.

**Fix:** Restore the action and, when relevant, the actor. Keep nominalizations that are established domain terms or help the sentence focus on a concept.

### False agency

**Signal:** An abstraction appears to decide, believe, reward, demand, or act, hiding who is responsible.

**Fix:** Name the person or institution when responsibility matters.

**Keep when:** The inanimate subject is accurate conventional shorthand and no responsibility is obscured, such as a report containing a result or a compiler rejecting code.

### Synonym cycling

**Signal:** The same entity receives several labels only to avoid repetition.

**Fix:** Repeat the clearest term. Keep distinct labels only when they mark distinct roles or states.

### Empty modifier

**Signal:** An adjective or adverb intensifies a claim but supplies no scale, comparison, manner, or stance.

**Fix:** Cut it, choose a more exact verb or noun, or add supported measurement.

**Keep when:** It changes truth conditions, degree, chronology, attitude, or voice.

### Decorative analogy

Use an analogy only when it makes an unfamiliar idea easier to understand, remains accurate at the relevant level, and is shorter than the literal explanation. Delete it when it merely makes a weak claim vivid. Do not impose a universal quota.

## Rhythm and format patterns

### Robotic rhythm

**Signal:** Consecutive sentences share length and syntax, every paragraph has the same footprint, or punchy fragments accumulate.

**Fix:** Rebuild the passage around the actual relationship among ideas. Do not vary length randomly.

### Punctuation mannerism

**Signal:** Dashes, colons, parentheses, semicolons, ellipses, or fragments repeat as a performance device.

**Fix:** Keep only the marks that clarify grammar, relation, or intentional cadence. Do not swap one crutch for another.

### Decorative formatting

**Signal:** Tiny sections receive headings, ordinary words receive bold emphasis, or bullets and emojis decorate rather than organize.

**Fix:** Let hierarchy and format match the information. Keep required templates, scannable lists, accessibility cues, and platform conventions.

### Chatbot residue

**Signal:** Unrequested praise, ritual reassurance, generic offers to help, or celebratory diagnosis surrounds the answer.

**Fix:** Respond to the substance. Keep courtesy that fits the relationship and medium.

## Frequent rule conflicts

| Suspected tell | Overcorrection | Contextual rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adverb | Delete every adverb | Delete only empty or propping modifiers |
| Passive voice | Force a human subject into every sentence | Prefer active when actor or responsibility matters |
| Fragment | Turn every fragment into a full sentence | Keep clear, intentional, voice-bearing fragments |
| Contrast | Ban every negative-positive form | Keep necessary, concrete, supported contrast |
| Three-item list | Change the count | Use the natural count |
| Dash or parenthesis | Ban the mark | Fix repeated decorative use |
| Metaphor | Remove all figurative language | Keep accurate analogies that reduce explanation cost |
| Hedge | Make the claim categorical | Preserve evidence-calibrated uncertainty |
| Correlation or sequence | Rewrite it as a cause | Preserve the supported evidence relation |
| Parallelism | Break every repeated shape | Keep grammatical parallelism; cut slogan symmetry |
| Topic sentence | Begin every paragraph the same way | Orient the reader only where needed |
